# Module 1: Security fundamentals and everyday risk

**Learning time:** 45–60 minutes. **Outcome:** understand and apply this protective capability in an authorised home or small-business setting.

## Understand the threat and control

Cybersecurity protects confidentiality (only authorised people can view data), integrity (data stays accurate) and availability (services work when needed). A threat is a possible cause of harm; a vulnerability is a weakness; risk considers likelihood and impact. Identify critical assets such as accounts, payment details and business files. A control reduces risk but rarely removes it. Understand that attackers often target people and simple mistakes rather than sophisticated technical flaws.

## Practical case

A fictional café runs a shared laptop, online bookings and a payment provider. List the assets, threats, weaknesses and controls. Rank risks using a simple low/medium/high matrix.

## Guided exercise

1. List three assets and owners; mark one threat and control per asset; explain why a password on a shared account cannot guarantee traceability.
2. Create a simple table with **risk, evidence, control, owner and review date**.
3. Model a successful authorised action and an unauthorised or failed action; explain how the control responds.
4. Record observations, assumptions and recommended next steps separately.

## Avoid these mistakes

Treat a vulnerability label as an automatic incident only after investigating exposure and effects.

## Knowledge checkpoint

1. Explain the primary control and the harm it reduces.
2. What evidence would prove the control worked, and what would trigger escalation?

## Extended learning: assess risks rather than memorise threats

**Confidentiality** asks who may read data, **integrity** whether the data remains correct, and **availability** whether authorised users can access it when needed. A risk statement should identify the asset, threat event, weakness, impact and owner. For a fictional café, consider the booking laptop, business email and payment administration accounts.

| Asset | Example threat | Vulnerability | Protective measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Booking laptop | Lost or stolen device | No screen lock or encryption | Device lock, encryption and remote management |
| Business mailbox | Account takeover | Reused password | Unique credentials and phishing-resistant MFA |
| Booking records | Accidental deletion | No tested recovery | Versioned backup and restore verification |

### Worked tabletop exercise

1. Define the three assets and their operational importance.
2. For each, explain **one realistic event** that would interrupt service or expose information.
3. Estimate likelihood and impact as low, medium or high; state the assumptions behind each rating.
4. Choose an affordable control, responsible owner and review date.
5. Test a permitted success case and a blocked/failed case using a fictional setup.
6. Record residual risk: a control reduces exposure but does not guarantee safety.

**Example:** A shared mailbox may allow a legitimate cashier to read booking enquiries, but individual identities are still needed to establish who performed an action. Least privilege and activity records make investigation possible.

**Knowledge transfer:** Why can a technically secure system still fail its availability objective? Describe a backup, support or continuity control that addresses the gap.
