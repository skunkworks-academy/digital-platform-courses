# Module 2: Phishing, scams and social engineering

**Learning time:** 45–60 minutes. **Outcome:** understand and apply this protective capability in an authorised home or small-business setting.

## Understand the threat and control

Phishing attempts to trick a person into sharing information or performing an action. Smishing is via text, vishing via voice, and business email compromise impersonates an authorised colleague or supplier. Common signals include urgency, mismatched domains, changed bank details and unexpected attachments. Legitimate looking logos and HTTPS do not prove sender legitimacy. Verify unusual requests using a known independently obtained contact channel.

## Practical case

A message says 'Your manager needs you to update a supplier bank account in the next 10 minutes.' The correct response is not to reply to that message: phone the approved supplier contact from company records, apply change approval, and record the incident.

## Guided exercise

1. Inspect five simulated messages. Identify claimed sender, requested action, urgency and how to verify it without using the supplied link.
2. Create a simple table with **risk, evidence, control, owner and review date**.
3. Model a successful authorised action and an unauthorised or failed action; explain how the control responds.
4. Record observations, assumptions and recommended next steps separately.

## Avoid these mistakes

No test requires clicking a real suspicious link; use offline examples and fabricated domains such as example.invalid.

## Knowledge checkpoint

1. Explain the primary control and the harm it reduces.
2. What evidence would prove the control worked, and what would trigger escalation?

## Extended learning: detect and verify suspicious requests

Phishing attempts to trick someone into disclosing information or taking an unsafe action. **Smishing** uses SMS, **vishing** uses telephone calls, and business email compromise can impersonate a colleague or supplier. A familiar logo or an HTTPS padlock does **not** prove a message is legitimate. Inspect the *requested behaviour*: urgency, secrecy, unexpected credentials or changes to bank details are warning signs.

### Worked example: a changed supplier account

A fictional message asks a finance assistant to pay an urgent invoice to a new beneficiary.

1. **Pause the payment.** Do not click the link or use the phone number supplied in the suspicious message.
2. **Compare context.** Check the purchase order and the previously verified supplier details.
3. **Contact independently.** Call the supplier using an existing trusted record, not the new contact information.
4. **Escalate appropriately.** Follow the organisation's documented change-of-bank-details procedure.
5. **Preserve minimal evidence.** Record the sender, time, request and decision through the approved reporting process.

### Recognise common manipulation

| Signal | What it tries to cause | Safer response |
|---|---|---|
| “Act in ten minutes” | Bypass normal approval | Check the urgency independently |
| “Keep this private” | Avoid peer verification | Consult an authorised reviewer |
| “Verify your password here” | Harvest credentials | Open the legitimate service directly |
| “Use our new account” | Divert payment | Confirm via an established channel |

**Practice:** Write a calm, three-sentence response to a fictional suspicious request that avoids accusation, prevents an unsafe action and explains the verification step. Never forward live malicious attachments to learners.
