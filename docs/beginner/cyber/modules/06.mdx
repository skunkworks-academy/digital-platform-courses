# Module 6: Incident reporting, privacy and recovery

**Learning time:** 45–60 minutes. **Outcome:** understand and apply this protective capability in an authorised home or small-business setting.

## Understand the threat and control

Common incident signals include unexpected MFA prompts, changed billing details, unknown logins, ransomware notes and data sent to an unexpected recipient. A safe response is to stop risky actions, preserve relevant evidence, contact the designated helpdesk/security officer, contain the impact using authorised procedures, and recover only after validation. Record time, affected system, what happened, action, owner and lessons learned. Regulatory notification duties depend on jurisdiction, organisation and facts; do not promise blanket deadlines.

## Practical case

A fictional employee accidentally shares an internal file with a public link. They report immediately, revoke exposure with an authorised owner, preserve audit information and assess whether there was actual access.

## Guided exercise

1. Write a first-response card containing who to contact, what not to delete, containment approvals, user communications and recovery steps.
2. Create a simple table with **risk, evidence, control, owner and review date**.
3. Model a successful authorised action and an unauthorised or failed action; explain how the control responds.
4. Record observations, assumptions and recommended next steps separately.

## Avoid these mistakes

Run an incident tabletop with a timeline; identify three evidence items and one post-incident preventive improvement.

## Knowledge checkpoint

1. Explain the primary control and the harm it reduces.
2. What evidence would prove the control worked, and what would trigger escalation?

## Extended learning: respond without destroying evidence

An incident is an event that threatens information or service. Not every suspicious message is a confirmed compromise, so separate **observation**, **hypothesis** and **verified impact**. Incident response commonly moves through preparation, identification, containment, eradication, recovery and lessons learned. A beginner should follow approved reporting paths rather than improvising invasive remediation.

### Worked example: unexpected account activity

A fictional employee notices unfamiliar sign-ins and a file-sharing permission they did not approve.

1. Note what was observed, when and through which approved tool.
2. Report the issue to the authorised security or IT contact immediately.
3. Avoid deleting logs or performing unauthorised searches for an attacker.
4. Follow approved session-revocation, password recovery and MFA review steps.
5. Review shared links and affected information **within authorised access**.
6. Preserve a concise timeline of decisions and actions; disclose only necessary details.

### Privacy and recovery decisions

| Question | Good practice |
|---|---|
| What information might be affected? | Classify data and ask the incident owner to determine scope |
| Who must be told? | Follow the legal, contractual and organisational notification process |
| How do services resume? | Restore from known-good configuration or backup, then verify |
| What reduces recurrence? | Address the root control gap and test the improvement |

**Practice:** Write a fictional incident report containing a timestamp, observed alert, estimated impact, immediate safe action, escalation contact and next review. Do not claim legal breach notification thresholds without consulting applicable guidance.
