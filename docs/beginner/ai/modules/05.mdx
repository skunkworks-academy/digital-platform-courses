# Module 5: Working with data and simple calculations

**Time:** about 45–60 minutes. **Outcome:** The arithmetic reconciles, identified data-quality errors have documented treatment, and the narrative avoids causal claims unsupported by data.

## Learn: core concepts

An AI assistant can propose spreadsheet formulas, cleaning steps, summaries and charts, but arithmetic and labels must be verified in the source workbook. Explain types: text, number, date, category. Use formulas for deterministic arithmetic and inspect totals, duplicates, blanks and outliers. Models can misread tables or confuse currency and tax treatment. A chart is only trustworthy when its axis, denominator, period and grouping are accurate.

## Realistic workplace scenario

For fictional weekly ticket counts 12, 18, 15 and 20, a reviewer calculates total 65 and average 16.25 with a spreadsheet before accepting an AI narrative.

## Guided activity

1. Create a five-row synthetic expense list with category, description, quantity, unit price and amount.
2. Ask an assistant to propose formulas to calculate total by item and by category; enter them in a spreadsheet or verify manually.
3. Include one blank, one duplicate and one incorrect currency format, then record how you detect and correct each.
4. Produce a one-paragraph narrative that says what the dataset does and does not prove.

## Verify your result

The arithmetic reconciles, identified data-quality errors have documented treatment, and the narrative avoids causal claims unsupported by data. Produce a brief validation table with columns **expected result**, **actual result**, **evidence**, **decision** and **remediation**. The observed result must match the stated purpose without accidental disclosure or unapproved action.

:::warning Responsible practice
Never let a model directly approve financial transactions or publish numeric business claims without independent controls.
:::

## Extended learning: AI-assisted data reasoning

### Separate arithmetic, analysis and explanation

A model may describe a correct formula while calculating the wrong result. Use a spreadsheet or calculator as the **independent numerical source of truth**, and treat the model's narrative as a draft interpretation. For data work, inspect units, missing values, duplicates and the denominator used for percentages. A change over time is not proof that a particular intervention caused it.

### Worked example: a small training register

The fictional table below shows completed training seats:

| Course | Planned | Completed | Completion rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| IT fundamentals | 20 | 16 | 80% |
| Cybersecurity | 15 | 12 | 80% |
| Spreadsheet basics | 10 | 9 | 90% |
| **Total** | **45** | **37** | **82.2%** |

The **overall** rate is 37 divided by 45, approximately 82.2%; it is not necessarily the average of the three percentages. Recalculate with a spreadsheet formula or calculator and explain why weighting matters. Now imagine that the second row was imported twice: the totals may look plausible but would describe a different population.

### A repeatable verification sequence

1. State the question: “What proportion of planned seats were completed?”
2. Define every field and identify whether rows are independent records.
3. Detect duplicates and blanks; describe how they will be treated before deleting anything.
4. Calculate totals and rates with visible formulas.
5. Check a sample by hand, and reconcile the grand total with the source.
6. Draft a short conclusion that reports the result without claiming why it happened.

**Practice:** Increase the completed value for spreadsheet basics from 9 to 10. Recompute both the course rate and overall rate. Explain which claims the new data **does not** support, such as proving that a new learning platform caused improvement.
