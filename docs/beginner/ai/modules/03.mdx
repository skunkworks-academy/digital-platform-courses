# Module 3: Fact-checking and responsible research

**Time:** about 45–60 minutes. **Outcome:** A corrected brief must clearly separate observations, assumptions, and unsupported claims; every consequential fact has traceable evidence.

## Learn: core concepts

Language models may hallucinate fabricated references, policy details, quotations and statistics. An answer needs a verifiable source and a relevant publication date where facts are changing. Separate first-party documentation, reputable secondary analysis and unsupported opinion. Quotes, prices and regulatory obligations should be checked against originals. When researching a rapidly changing technology, record the date of verification and the product version.

## Realistic workplace scenario

A model claims a particular cloud service is free worldwide. The reviewer opens the current vendor pricing page, verifies regional eligibility and records terms before quoting a customer.

## Guided activity

1. Ask an approved AI assistant to list three features of a named workplace tool and cite official documentation.
2. Open the corresponding official vendor pages manually, then mark each assertion verified, contradicted or unknown.
3. Correct the generated summary and add source URL and checked date to each material claim.
4. Document two circumstances in which you would decline to publish the answer.

## Verify your result

A corrected brief must clearly separate observations, assumptions, and unsupported claims; every consequential fact has traceable evidence. Produce a brief validation table with columns **expected result**, **actual result**, **evidence**, **decision** and **remediation**. The observed result must match the stated purpose without accidental disclosure or unapproved action.

:::warning Responsible practice
Do not treat a plausible-looking URL, fabricated citation or AI-generated confidence score as independent verification.
:::

## Extended learning: evidence, provenance and verification

### Know the difference between a claim and its proof

A generated answer can contain a correct fact for the wrong reason. **Provenance** means knowing where evidence originated and how it was transformed. For high-impact facts, prefer the primary authority (for example, the examination provider's published requirements) and check dates, versions and applicable regions. An online summary may be useful for discovery but should not automatically become the authority.

| Review question | Evidence to look for | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Who published it? | Named authoritative owner | Anonymous repost with no source |
| When did it apply? | Publication or effective date | A stale, undated snapshot |
| Does it support the claim? | Exact rule, figure or definition | A link that discusses a different topic |
| Can we reproduce it? | Source document and review steps | “The AI said so” |

### Worked example: conflicting course information

A trainee finds two webpages with different certification exam requirements. First record the **exact claim** on each page. Next locate the current official examination guidance, noting its region and revision. If an official answer is not available, label the result **unverified** and ask the appropriate owner for confirmation. Do not average the conflicting requirements or present a guess as policy.

Use a short verification log:

| Claim | Evidence consulted | Confidence | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exam includes a practical component | Official provider guide, date recorded | High if current and in scope | Publish with a traceable source |
| Registration closes next week | Third-party blog only | Unverified | Do not publish until confirmed |

### Practise source evaluation

Choose three **fictional** or publicly available claims. For each, distinguish an observation, an inference and a recommendation. Write a corrected paragraph that preserves what is known and explicitly identifies what remains uncertain. This is the foundation of responsible AI-assisted research.
